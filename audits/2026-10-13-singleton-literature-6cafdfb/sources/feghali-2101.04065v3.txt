                                                      Kempe equivalence of 4-critical planar graphs
                                                                                      Carl Feghali∗




arXiv:2101.04065v3 [math.CO] 4 Jul 2022
                                                                                        Abstract
                                                       Answering a question of Mohar from 2007, we show that for every 4-critical planar
                                                    graph, its set of 4-colorings is a Kempe class.


                                          1         Introduction
                                          Let G be a graph, and let k be a non-negative integer. A (proper) k-coloring of G is a
                                          function ϕ : V (G) → {1, . . . , k} such that ϕ(u) 6= ϕ(v) whenever uv ∈ E(G).
                                              A Kempe chain in colors {a, b} is a maximal connected subgraph K of G such that
                                          every vertex of K has color a or b. By swapping the colors a and b on K, a new coloring
                                          is obtained. This operation is called a K-change. If c2 is a k-coloring obtained from a
                                          k-coloring c1 by a single K-change, then we write c1 ∼k c2 . Two k-colorings c1 and c2 are
                                          K-equivalent (or K k -equivalent) if c1 be obtained from c2 by a sequence of K-changes. A
                                          K-change is trivial if the Kempe chain under consideration consists of a single vertex.
                                              Let CGk be the set of k-colorings of G. The equivalence classes CGk / ∼k are called Kempe
                                          classes. The number of Kempe classes of G is denoted Kc(G, k).
                                              Kempe chains were introduced by Kempe in his failed attempt at proving the Four
                                          Colour Theorem. Nevertheless, they have proved to be one of the most useful tools in
                                          graph coloring theory. As pointed out by Mohar [16], the number of Kempe equivalence
                                          classes of colorings has some applications in statistical physics [17] and Markov chains [23].
                                          Furthermore, there are many results on determining whether all colorings of a graph are
                                          Kempe equivalent or not whenever the graph belongs to a special graph class. For instance,
                                          Meyniel [14] showed that the set of 5-colorings of a planar graph forms a single Kempe
                                          class, and Las Vergnas and Meyniel [12] extended this result to K5 -minor-free graphs.
                                          Mohar [16] showed that the set of 4-colorings of every 3-colorable planar graph is a Kempe
                                          class, and asked whether his result can be extended to 4-critical planar graphs (a graph G
                                          is 4-critical if it is not 3-colorable, but every proper subgraph of G is 3-colorable). In that
                                          same paper, Mohar also conjectured that the set of k-colorings of a k-regular graph forms
                                          a Kempe class, and this was settled in [2, 9]. For further details and examples, we refer
                                          the reader to [4, 5, 7, 16, 22]. The edge-coloring version has also been considered in several

    In this note, we answer Mohar’s aforementioned problem in the positive.
Theorem 1. Let G be a 4-critical planar graph. Then Kc(G, 4) = 1.
  We should note that Theorem 1 is best possible in the sense that there are infinitely
many planar graphs with more than one Kempe equivalence class as shown by Mohar [15].


2     Preliminaries
In this section, we gather some of the necessary tools to establish Theorem 1. The first
lemma is implicit in [16].
Lemma 1 (Mohar [16, Proposition 2.4]). Let k ≥ 1 be an integer, G be a graph, and v a
vertex of G with degree strictly less than k. If Kc(G − v, k) = 1, then Kc(G, k) = 1.
Lemma 2 (Mohar [16, Lemma 4.2]). Suppose that G is a subgraph of a graph G0 . Let
c01 , c02 be r-colorings of G0 . For i ∈ {1, 2}, denote by ci the restriction of c0i to G. If c01 and
c02 are K r -equivalent, then c1 and c2 are K r -equivalent colorings of G.
Lemma 3 (Mohar [16, Theorem 4.4]). For every 3-colorable planar graph G, Kc(G, 4) = 1.
   Let G be a planar graph, and let S ⊆ V (G). Two 4-colorings c1 , c2 of G are said to be
KS -equivalent if there exists a sequence of K-changes from c1 to c2 that does not change
the color of a vertex in S to color 4. We have the following proposition.
Proposition 1. Let G = (V, E) be a 3-colorable triangulation of the plane. Then every
4-coloring of G is KV -equivalent to the (unique) 3-coloring of G.
    The proof of Proposition 1 follows, nearly word by word, from the proof of Theorem 1
in [10] by Fisk. We include some of the details for the convenience of the reader. First, we
require some definitions and an auxiliary lemma.
    Let f be a 4-coloring of a triangulation G, and let e = xy be an edge of G. By the
color of e under f , we mean {f (x), f (y)}. Two triangles xyz and xyw contain the edge
e. If f (w) = f (z) then e is called singular, and if f (w) 6= f (z) then e is nonsingular. We
require the following result of Fisk extracted from the proof of Theorem 1 in [10].
Lemma 4 (Fisk [10]). Let G be a 3-colorable triangulation of the plane, and let f be a
4-coloring of G. If G contains a nonsingular edge e under f and S denotes the set of
nonsingular edges of G having the same color as e, then S contains a cycle that bounds
some region of the plane.
Proof of Proposition 1. Given a 4-coloring f of G, we show how to obtain a new coloring
g that is KV -equivalent to f and with fewer nonsingular edges. Note that each edge in the
unique 3-coloring of G is singular, and thus, by repetition, this will prove the proposition.

                                                 2
    We first show that for every v ∈ V (G) with f (v) = 4, either v is incident with a
nonsingular edge or we may change the color of v to a color distinct from 4 by exhibiting
a trivial K-change. To see this, note that since G is a triangulation and is 3-colorable, the
neighborhood of v induces an even cycle C. If |f (C)| = 2, i.e., there is a color a ∈ {1, 2, 3}
not used by f on C, then we may change the color of v to a. Otherwise, f (C) = {1, 2, 3}
and, by the pigeonhole principle, some edge incident with v is nonsingular, as needed.
    To prove the proposition, we can thus safely assume that there is a nonsingular edge e
under f having color {a, 4} for some a ∈ {1, 2, 3}. By Lemma 4, there is a cycle in G whose
edges have the same color as e and bounding some region D of the plane. By interchanging
the two colors in {1, 2, 3} \ {a} in the interior of D, we obtain a new coloring g with fewer
nonsingular edges than f (the edges in the interior of D stay singular or nonsingular, while
the edges of the cycle on the boundary of D change from nonsingular to singular). This
completes the proof.
    We will also need the following result of Mohar. A plane graph is a near-triangulation
if every facial cycle of the graph is a triangle except possibly for the outer cycle.

Proposition 2 (Mohar [16, Proposition 4.3]). Suppose that G is a planar graph with a
facial cycle C. If c1 , c2 are 4-colorings of G, then there is a near-triangulation T of the
plane with the outer cycle C such that T ∩ G = C and there are 4-colorings c01 , c02 of G
that are K-equivalent to c1 and c2 , respectively, such that c01 and c02 can be extended to
4-colorings of T ∪ G. Moreover, if the restriction of c1 to C is a 3-coloring, then c01 = c1 ,
and c1 can be extended to a 3-coloring of T ∪ G.


3     The proof of Theorem 1
In this section, we prove Theorem 1. We begin with some lemmas.

Lemma 5 (Koester [11]). Every 4-critical planar graph has a vertex of degree at most 4.

    For a graph G, a subgraph H of G and a coloring ϕ of G, let ϕ  H denote the restriction
of ϕ to H. For a vertex v of G of degree exactly 4 and a 4-coloring f of G, we say that f is
v-good if f colors exactly two neighbors of v alike; in other words, if all four colors appear
in the closed neighborhood of v under f .

Lemma 6. Let G be a 4-critical planar graph, let v be a vertex of degree exactly four in
G, and let ϕ be a 4-coloring of G. Then ϕ is K-equivalent to a v-good 4-coloring ψ of G.

Proof. Since G is 4-critical, the graph H = G − v is 3-colorable. By Lemma 3, there is a
sequence of K-changes ϕ  H = c01 ∼4 · · · ∼4 c0m in H where c0m is a 3-coloring; moreover,
c0m uses all three colors in the neighborhood of v else G would be 3-colorable.
    Thus, we may choose t ∈ {1, . . . , m} to be the smallest index such that c0t colors exactly
two neighbors of v alike. Now, the same argument as in the proof of Lemma 1 can be applied
to show that the sequence of K-changes ϕ  H = c01 ∼4 · · · ∼4 c0t extends to a sequence of

    For j ∈ {1, . . . , t}, we extend c0j to a 4-coloring cj of G as follows. Observe that

neighbors colored alike and has degree 4 and so there is a color a not appearing in its closed
neighborhood. Thus, by first preceding this K-change by changing the color of v to a (via
a trivial K-change), the result follows.

      We are now ready to prove Theorem 1.
Proof of Theorem 1. Suppose G has a vertex v of degree three or less. Since G is 4-critical,
G − v is 3-colorable. By Lemma 3, Kc(G − v, 4) = 1. By Lemma 1, Kc(G, 4) = 1. So we
can assume that every vertex of G has degree at least four. By Lemma 5, G has a vertex v
of degree exactly four. Let ϕ1 and ϕ2 be 4-coloring of G. To prove the theorem, it suffices
to show that ϕ1 and ϕ2 are K-equivalent.
    By Lemma 6, we may assume that ϕ1 and ϕ2 are v-good 4-colorings of G. Since G is
4-critical, there is a v-good 4-coloring ψ of G such that ψ  (G − v) is a 3-coloring. By
Lemma 7, for i ∈ {1, 2} ϕi is K-equivalent to ψ. Thus, ϕ1 is K-equivalent to ϕ2 and the
theorem is proved.

4    Final remarks
Meyniel [14] showed that the set of 5-colorings of a planar graph is a Kempe class. We
propose the following generalization. A list assignment of a graph is a function L that
assigns to each vertex v a list L(v) of colors. The graph G is L-colorable if it has a proper
coloring f such that f (v) ∈ L(v) for each vertex v of G.
Conjecture 1. Let G be a planar graph, and L be a list assignment for G such that
|L(v)| ≥ 5 for each v ∈ V (G). Then the set of L-colorings of G forms a Kempe class.
    Note that the set of L-colorings of G in the statement of Conjecture 1 is non-empty by
a celebrated result of Thomassen [20].
    A fundamental theorem of Grötzsch states that triangle-free planar graphs are 3-
colorable. Towards a Kempe analogue Grötzsch’s theorem, Salas and Sokal [19] recently
asked if Kc(G, 3) = 1 for every triangle-free planar graph G. We believe this to be true.
Conjecture 2. If G is a triangle-free planar graph, then Kc(G, 3) = 1.
    There are triangle-free planar graphs G and a list assignment L for G such that |L(v)| =
3 for each v ∈ V (G) with the property that G is not L-colorable. However, if G has girth
at least 5, then G is always L-colorable [21].
Conjecture 3. Let G be a planar graph of girth 5, and L a list assignment for G such
that |L(v)| ≥ 3 for each v ∈ V (G). Then the set of L-colorings of G forms a Kempe class.
